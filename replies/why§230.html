I'll try to answer your question the best I can. But your question has two ambiguities. These might contribute to the answer that you get having a gap with what you intend.

The word "Should" brings in Ambiguities

The word "should" has a spectrum of meanings. It is a marker for expectations, wishes, that which aligns with one's values, conforming to some set of rules, and a few more things. For less ambiguity, I would encourage generally avoiding the use of the word altogether.

§230 isn't specific to Social Media

§230 is not limited to social media, but is available to any user (like you and me) or provider of an interactive computer service. These are inclusive of social media along with online gaming, customer reviews, blogs, email services, wikis, and a lot more. If your question is meant to specifically exclude social media services from these protections that runs into conflicts with equal protections under the law. As the state of Florida discovered in NetChoice v Moody, there is not a bright line separating social media from services that are not social media.

Legacy Media also has protections

Legacy media is not without protections for speech from others. These protections go back to the telegraph. I've mentioned some of the other protections already. But here again.

  • Wire Service Defense - If a org republishes a story from another news org, generally only the originating news org has liability if the story was found to be defamatory. This is a bit like §230, in that the distributer of some item may be protected from liability for the words of the original party that published the story.
  • The Smith v California Defense - This defense came about because it was unreasonable to a book distributer to be aware of everything said in all the books and filter the available books based on their words. Putting such a liability on book distributers would have a speech chilling effect, and suppress the expressions of good and bad speakers alike.
  • Radio Defense - This came about from the case of Summit Hotel Co. v. Nat'l Broadcasting Co. I encourage checking this case out, as it has overlap with your question about Fox News bringing on a guest. In this case, a guest made a statement alleged to be defamatory. The broadcasting station was taken to court. There were a couple of layers of separation between the broadcasting station and the speech. The station leased its facilities to another party (J. Walter Thompson Company), and that party invited the guest. Additionally, the words were not part of the script or uttered in the rehersals. On appeal, the court did not agree with the broadcaster being held liable without any fault. It lacked intent to broadcast a defamatory statement and the interjection was made under circumstances that precluded anticipation or prevention. The court also didn't think it would be reasonable for the broadcaster to have an expert in law to detect in real time decide what utterences are or are not defamation.

    From the Summit Case we have Against the few exceptional situations in which absolute liability may have been applied, stands the great weight of modern authority that no man should be held liable for an unintentional injury resulting from the performance of a lawful act without negligence or wilful misconduct. Compare this to the Dominion v Newsmax case where the judge said "Newsmax either knew its statements about Domionion's role in the election fraud were false or had a high defree of awareness that they were false."

A Less Ambiguous question

I'm trying to make a good-faith attempt to address what I think you are asking. A key attribute in online publication is the volume of information and the efforts that would be necessary to ensure that all statements published are lawful. We find the least amount of protections on a party that published some statement in question, since it is a reasonable expectation that a person can account for the lawfulness of their own words. This expecation remains when an entity is publishing the words of its agents, as we ususally see occur in newspapers. But for the distribution of media, it isn't considered reasonable to expect a distributer to validate the words and statements that it is distributing for lawfulness. News stands, book redistributers, and even publishers of books written by others have all been protected from liability for the words of the author. This is contrary to some of your earlier comments, which appear to have been made with the belief that these distributers have no defense at all. §230(c)(1) is yet another distributer liability protection. It isn't the only instance of a law of this type.

I assume that you don't expect Twitter to read and analyze 500,000,000 tweets per day for lawfulness, or for YouTube to analyze 72,000 hours of video per day, or for an email provider to do the same for all emails passing through their system, or a website posting reviews, so on. No one has proposed a reasonable set of actions that they expect of a distributer of speech online to avoid liability.

Without the protection of §230, there are a lot of services that we enjoy that become high risk. I find it useful to be able to read what other people have written about a product or service. Holding liable the host of such exchanges for any expression alleged to be problematic would likely result in those exchanges simply not being available.

Removing the protection of §230 also hands a very effective power of veto for speech over to those wealth or with enough resources. They could simply allege that some item of speech is defamatory to force its removal. Keep in mind that part of how §230 became a law was that the Wolf of Wallstreet alleged that a statement posted about his firm being engaged in fraud was defamatory (spoiler, his firm **was** engaged in fraud!). If you are not familiar with the back story, you can find it in audio format here. There are some politicians and other wealthy people that would love to take advantage of the higher risk environment to control speech.
